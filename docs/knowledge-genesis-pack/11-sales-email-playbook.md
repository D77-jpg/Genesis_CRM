# Genesis Bags — Sales Email Playbook for First Outreach and Follow-up

Source: Genesis sales process and website guidance; this is a writing playbook, not a supplier fact card. Facts used in an email must still come from approved knowledge.

## The core principle

Write one relevant, verifiable idea for this customer, not a catalogue. A good first email should feel like it was written after looking at the customer's product and packaging, not like it was sent to a list.

## First outreach structure

1. **Greeting:** use the contact's first name (for example "Dear Dave,"); if no contact name, use the company name plus "team". Never use "Dear there", "Dear Sir/Madam", "Dear Customer" or "Dear All".
2. **Customer-specific hook (1 sentence):** a verifiable fact from the customer's website, profile or authorized history. If the website was not read, use the industry/product from the profile and do not pretend to have seen the site.
3. **Relevance bridge (1–2 sentences):** connect the customer's product to one or two capabilities that are actually in approved knowledge. Use the exact material/structure/logotype language where possible. Do not list every category.
4. **Credible next step (1 sentence):** offer a specific, low-friction next step, for example two or three structure options sized around their product, or a sample swatch comparison.
5. **One clear question:** ask for the one missing detail that would let you prepare the next step. Do not ask three or four questions in one email.
6. **Sign-off:** the system appends the approved signature; do not write a duplicate signature.

Target 110–170 words, 3–4 short paragraphs. Each factual paragraph should carry one independently verifiable idea, not a bundle of product capabilities. This lets review keep the supported parts if a detail needs correction.

## Personalization rules

- Customer website facts are **customer descriptions**, not proof of Genesis capability: write "Your website shows…" in its own paragraph, never mixed with "we make…".
- Customer profile facts can personalize the opening, but cannot become Genesis product claims.
- Customer requests, target prices, competitor references and purchase interest are **customer-side context**, not our commitments.
- Name the customer's product specifically: "your coin pendants and initial necklaces" is better than "your jewelry products".
- If the website read failed or there is no website snapshot, say less; do not invent a studio, founder, product line or purchasing intent.
- Existing conversation: do not write as if this is the first contact; reference the previous subject/date only if it is in the authorized history.

## Evidence discipline

- Every specific capability or number in an email must be traceable to a published knowledge entry or an approved quotation/agreement.
- If a product is not covered by published knowledge, omit the detail and ask a question instead of filling space with generic claims.
- For price, MOQ, lead time, payment, certification or exclusivity, use only the approved commercial-policy entry and the written quotation. When not confirmed, say "I will confirm" rather than giving a range as a commitment.
- Do not add inserts, trays, accessories, sampling promises, testing, capacity or lead times unless they are in the knowledge entry.
- Do not convert a customer's requested quantity, target price or launch date into a Genesis commitment.

## Follow-up emails

- Follow-up 1: one new angle or one useful proof point, plus a low-pressure question. Do not repeat the whole introduction.
- Follow-up 2: a short, specific offer (sample swatch, structure comparison) or a graceful close. Do not manufacture urgency or claim the customer is interested.
- Reference the previous email date/subject only when it is in the authorized history.
- If the customer has replied, switch from outreach to reply mode: answer their actual question first, then add one next step.
- If there is an opt-out, rejection, bounce or complaint signal, stop marketing follow-ups immediately.

## Useful first-email patterns

Pattern A — product-specific structure idea:
"Your website shows [customer product]. For a piece like that, we could compare [one relevant structure] with [one alternative] and size the insert around your piece. Would it help if I sent two options with dimensions?"

Pattern B — material/logo swatch:
"Your [product line] could suit [material]. We can prepare a swatch showing [one relevant logo method] on [material]. Which finish do you want to compare first?"

Pattern C — packaging suite:
"You currently present [product] in [observed packaging]. We could map a coordinated [pouch + box + accessory] set so the unboxing stays consistent. Would a one-page structure comparison be useful?"

Use these as structural patterns, not as reusable claims. Replace bracketed parts with verified customer and knowledge facts.

## What to avoid

- "We are a leading manufacturer" with no specific relevance.
- Listing ten categories, materials and certifications in one email.
- Generic flattery ("I love your brand") without a fact.
- Claiming a website detail you did not actually read.
- Promising free samples, free tooling, a price, a lead time or certification without checking policy.
- Writing only "your website + one question" with no useful next step.
- Writing a long product paragraph that mixes many claims; one unsupported detail can cause the whole paragraph to be removed at review.
- Sending automatically: every email remains a human-reviewed draft.
